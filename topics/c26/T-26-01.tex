\topic{T-26-01}{The Ingratitude Cure}{Helping people banks nothing; structuring their need banks everything}

\begin{core}
The belief that constant goodness and sacrifice purchase loyalty is the first casualty of dealing with people. Benefits received become entitlements; the helped party's lasting feeling is not gratitude but grievance that the help was not larger. If your good deeds are to bank anything, the aftermath has to be structured: never let a person settle into receiving, and keep what they receive tied to what they need from you.
\end{core}
\begin{mechanism}
The source's first year in business was a seminar in ingratitude. He nursed a drunk toward recovery; the man repaid him by pursuing his woman friend. He staked a broke acquaintance's start-up --- capital, his own apartment --- and the night the lease transferred, he was given one hour to clear out, plus a swaggered promise never to see the money still owed. The pattern is not bad luck; it is the schedule. Kindness delivered every time becomes the new baseline, and the baseline feels earned. The cure is the intermittent schedule applied after the fact: vary what you give, size it unpredictably, and let the counterpart's need of you do the work that their gratitude was supposed to do. Gratitude flows from need, not from benefits --- a person who can predict your kindness stops pricing it, and a person who needs it cannot afford to be ungrateful.
\end{mechanism}
\begin{conditions}
Strongest in ongoing relationships with a history of one-way giving --- partnerships, mentorships, family firms, the friend you always rescue. Weakens in one-shot exchanges (where you simply never help), and where the recipient genuinely pays a market price --- paid help carries no debt and needs no gratitude.
\end{conditions}
\begin{application}
  \item Audit the ledger: for each close relationship, what has it repaid over two years? Inflow-only accounts are being farmed.
  \item Stop buying loyalty with sacrifice. Sacrifice buys entitlement with interest.
  \item When you do help, attach the help to structure --- what they need from you --- never to their character.
  \item After any large help, expect entitlement, and make the next benefit unpredictable in timing and size.
  \item When the ledger shows only outflow, close the account. Quietly, without a scene, without a loan.
\end{application}
\begin{feedback}
  \item Landing: the helped party's tone shifts from entitled to careful once the schedule varies --- the account has been repriced.
  \item Landing: they start offering something, anything, to keep the flow. Need has appeared where gratitude was advertised.
  \item Failing: each act of help becomes the opening bid for a larger one. You are training the farming.
  \item Failing: your help is now retold as their own achievement. The ledger is being rewritten.
\end{feedback}
\begin{failure}
Administered without judgement, the cure turns relationships into conditioning labs --- a friend managed on a variable schedule is a trained animal, not an ally, and the operator who sees entitlement everywhere stops being able to see affection anywhere. The audit mindset also misfires on the few relationships where giving was genuinely mutual and simply invisible; closing those accounts is a loss the ledger cannot show.
\end{failure}
\begin{countermeasures}
  \item Notice who re-tells your favours as their own work. The account is already being restated in their favour.
  \item Refuse help that arrives framed as owed. Entitlement declared early is the cheapest exit offer you will get.
  \item In your own receiving, settle fast and credit by name --- paid debts and named helpers cannot be weaponised as unacknowledged sacrifice.
  \item If someone's generosity arrives on a variable schedule sized to hook you, name the training to yourself and the compulsion weakens (T-11-01).
\end{countermeasures}

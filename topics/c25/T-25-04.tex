\topic{T-25-04}{The Ackerman Plan}{A price ladder that ends on a number that looks calculated}

\begin{core}
A four-step haggle: set the target privately, open at 65 per cent of it, raise through 85 and 95 to 100, and close on a precise, non-round number plus a small non-monetary item. The shrinking raises read as approaching a real limit, and the odd final figure reads as calculation --- so the endpoint lands as a derived fact rather than a position.
\end{core}
\begin{mechanism}
The system beats the default haggle outcome, meeting in the middle --- a midpoint that, as T-25-03 shows, is the average of two anchors. Each part does one job. The 65 per cent opener leaves four moves of room while staying inside the defensible extreme. The decreasing increments --- twenty points, then ten, then five --- make the counterpart read a curve approaching a wall: each raise costs the raiser visibly more, so the next one is presumed to be the last. Between raises, empathy and varied forms of \emph{no} force the counterpart to counter first, so the ladder never climbs unopposed. The precise final number --- 37,893, not 38,000 --- borrows the credibility of calculation: round numbers are chosen, odd numbers are derived. The throw-in, a non-monetary item they probably do not want, signals the absolute limit.
\end{mechanism}
\begin{conditions}
Strongest in one-shot, single-issue price talks --- purchases, salary, settlement --- where the counterpart has no data advantage and no ongoing relationship to price the ladder against. Weakens against a counterpart who knows the system (the curve becomes legible and steerable), in repeated dealings where an extreme opener taxes your reputation in every later round, and whenever the target itself is a guess: the percentages then measure nothing. Power asymmetry can collapse the ladder --- sometimes there is no fourth step.
\end{conditions}
\begin{application}
  \item Fix the target from outside data before contact, and write it down.
  \item Open at 65 per cent --- the defensible extreme of your real position.
  \item Absorb counters with labels and no-oriented questions (T-24-05); never raise first.
  \item Raise 85, then 95, then 100 --- visibly pained, and spaced so each reads as extracted, not offered.
  \item End on a precise non-round figure inside your target, add the throw-in you can afford, and stop talking.
\end{application}
\begin{feedback}
  \item Working: they keep countering between your steps --- the ladder is being climbed jointly.
  \item Working: their offers approach your target while yours are still below 95.
  \item Off-track: they accept before your third raise --- the ladder was too shallow and money was left.
  \item Off-track: stalemate at your 95. Hold; the next move is theirs, and taking it for them is bidding against yourself.
\end{feedback}
\begin{failure}
Against a counterpart who recognises the curve, every rung signals your target rather than concedes; the system hands over its steering. Run eagerly --- fast, unopposed raises --- it signals room and invites compression. An unresearched target makes the whole ladder theatre. And the throw-in misfires both ways: read as insult it sours the end-state, read as theatre it confirms that the number was performance all along.
\end{failure}
\begin{countermeasures}
  \item Name the curve when you see it: decreasing raises are a system, not exhaustion. Say so, and the system spends itself.
  \item Demand a reason with each raise. A ladder is justified rung by rung or it is exposed.
  \item Stop moving. A ladder needs your counters; one firm restatement and a silence starves it.
  \item Write your own walk-away before contact. The ladder's real payload is the anchor it installs in you (T-25-03).
\end{countermeasures}

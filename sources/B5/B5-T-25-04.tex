\dossier{B5}{T-25-04}{\bookshort{B5}}{ch. 9, pp. 249--280}

\begin{distilled}
  \item The Ackerman model (from Mike Ackerman, kidnap-for-ransom consulting; endorsed for general use by Howard Raiffa) is an offer-counteroffer system with defined steps: set the target price; open at 65 percent of it; calculate three raises of decreasing increments, to 85, 95 and 100 percent.
  \item Between raises, use empathy and different ways of saying no to make the other side counter before you increase --- the system forbids bidding against yourself.
  \item The final amount must be a precise, non-round number (37,893, not 38,000): it gives the figure credibility and weight, reading as calculated rather than chosen.
  \item On the final number, throw in a non-monetary item they probably do not want, to demonstrate you are at your limit.
  \item The chapter also reports survey data that blunt assertion is mostly counterproductive: the large majority of effective negotiators in the sample were cooperative in style, which is why the ladder is run through empathy rather than force.
  \item Named hazards: the spotlight effect (fearing your weaknesses are more visible than they are), the accusation audit before hard numbers, and the Friday-afternoon timing effect.
\end{distilled}

\begin{unique}
  \item The complete percentage sequence with its rationale: the decreasing increments are the mechanism --- the counterpart reads a curve approaching a wall, not a series of amounts.
  \item The throw-in as a limit signal: a deliberately undesired item proves the money is exhausted, which a plain no cannot.
  \item The no-ladder pairing: the ladder only works if the counterpart is made to counter first at every step, which is what the varied refusals are for.
\end{unique}

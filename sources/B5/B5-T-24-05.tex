\dossier{B5}{T-24-05}{\bookshort{B5}}{ch. 4, pp. 102--129}

\begin{distilled}
  \item The yes-funnel is described from the receiving end: a telemarketer's scripted flowchart of small questions \emph{designed to cut off your escape routes as it funnels you down a path with no exit but yes}, and the design produces defensiveness before the pitch lands.
  \item Saying no gives the counterpart safety and control: it stops the feeling of being moved, and a counterpart who can refuse stops defending and starts engaging.
  \item The prescribed questions are no-oriented: \emph{is now a bad time?} in place of \emph{do you have a few minutes?} --- the counterpart answers no and grants the meeting.
  \item The dead-contact revival is a single email line: \emph{have you given up on this project?} --- answered no, it reopens the negotiation with the counterpart's movement owned by them.
  \item Three kinds of yes are distinguished (counterfeit, confirmation, commitment), with yes-pressure producing the counterfeit kind that evaporates on contact with cost.
\end{distilled}

\begin{unique}
  \item The engineering rule for asks: build the first refusal into the approach, because a counterpart who has exercised refusal cheaply stops pricing refusal as catastrophe.
  \item The recognition instruction, stated for the operator but serving defence: any sequence of trivial yeses with exits quietly closing is the funnel, and breaking one small link collapses the structure.
  \item The honesty boundary: a no-invitation only works as many times as it has been true; a decorative exit is priced as a trap the second time.
\end{unique}

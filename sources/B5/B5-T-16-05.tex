\dossier{B5}{T-16-05}{\bookshort{B5}}{ch. 5, pp. 130--151}

\begin{distilled}
  \item The two words of the chapter title are \emph{that's right}, and the source treats reaching them as the pivotal event of a negotiation.
  \item The way there is the summary: a paraphrase of the counterpart's position --- facts, reasons, and the feelings underneath --- delivered back to them accurately.
  \item The contrast case is \emph{you're right}: named as the phrase people use to make someone go away, agreement in words only, and the source explicitly warns against accepting it as progress.
  \item The hostage case (a self-declared CIA agent held in the Philippines) is the chapter's spine: the counterpart's own story, summarised back, produced the moment he stopped bargaining with the negotiator's framing.
  \item The claim underneath: real movement begins only when the counterpart hears their situation stated correctly by the other side.
\end{distilled}

\begin{unique}
  \item The counterfeit meter: the pair \emph{that's right} / \emph{you're right} as a live diagnostic --- one signals felt agreement, the other signals the conversation has ended while the words continue.
  \item The sequencing rule: no ask before the milestone; introducing your demand before \emph{that's right} forfeits the frame the summary built.
  \item The recognition mechanism stated as why summaries persuade: commitment attaches to conclusions a person reaches, and a summary makes your framing the occasion for their conclusion.
\end{unique}

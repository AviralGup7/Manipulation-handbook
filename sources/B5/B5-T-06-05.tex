\dossier{B5}{T-06-05}{\bookshort{B5}}{ch. 8, pp. 216--248}

\begin{distilled}
  \item The 7--38--55 rule (attributed to Albert Mehrabian): only 7 percent of a message rides on the words, 38 on tone of voice, 55 on body language and face --- with the source itself noting the figures mainly concern situations where feelings and attitudes are in play.
  \item The Pinocchio effect: most people offer obvious telltale signs when lying --- not a growing nose, but close enough --- cited from a study of the components of lying.
  \item Three kinds of yes are distinguished: counterfeit (given to end pressure), confirmation (innocent affirmation of small facts), and commitment (the real agreement). Yes-pressure manufactures the counterfeit kind.
  \item The rule of three: get the counterpart to agree to the same thing three times in the same conversation, which triples the strength of the dynamic --- liars struggle to keep a story stable across three passes.
  \item The chapter's execution focus: guaranteeing follow-through requires reading all three channels, because the words alone routinely report agreement that the other channels have not signed.
\end{distilled}

\begin{unique}
  \item The yes taxonomy paired with the rule of three as one instrument: each re-confirmation must survive a re-framing, and inconsistency across the passes is the lie detector.
  \item The channel test as an execution tool rather than a lie catcher: agreement is not counted as real until tone and face have co-signed it.
\end{unique}

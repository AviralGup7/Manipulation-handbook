\dossier{B5}{T-14-07}{\bookshort{B5}}{ch. 3, pp. 70--101}

\begin{distilled}
  \item The method is called labeling: name the counterpart's emotion or stake as an observation, in observation stems --- \emph{it seems like}, \emph{it sounds like} --- never as a direct question about the person.
  \item The founding case: six hours of labeling the fears of three armed fugitives (getting killed, going to jail) kept a siege from firing a shot; the source credits labels with keeping the counterpart processing instead of reacting.
  \item The term given for the underlying stance is tactical empathy: understanding the counterpart's position and voicing it back, not sympathising with it.
  \item Labels defuse negatives and reinforce positives; a positive label makes the counterpart expand on what is working.
  \item The pre-emptive form is the accusation audit: list every negative thing the counterpart could think or say about you before they can, so each arrives pre-answered.
  \item The rule for silence is repeated from the mirror: after a label, stop talking and let the counterpart confirm, deny, or refine.
\end{distilled}

\begin{unique}
  \item The grammar constraint: labels are statements about the situation, never questions about the person, and the softening stem is mandatory --- the distance is what makes the label bearable to confirm.
  \item The accusation audit as frame-reversal: spoken first and without defence, your own worst framing converts the counterpart from prosecutor to reassurer.
  \item The wrong-label cost stated from the field: a mislabelling tells the counterpart you were performing understanding, which costs more than having said nothing.
\end{unique}

\dossier{B7}{T-23-08}{\bookshort{B7}}{chs. 10--11, 14 (pp. 70--103, 104--112)}

\begin{distilled}
  \item The answerer holds the inherent advantage: they know what you need to know, and you do not know what they want to hide --- question design is the questioner's only leveller.
  \item One issue per question: compound questions donate escape routes (either half can be answered while the other evaporates).
  \item Never telegraph the inventory: showing what you already know lets the subject prepare; in the famous thirty-minute interview the detectives revealed too much early and stayed on the back foot --- and the interview ended with no charges.
  \item Timing against psychological entrenchment: the subject arrives with a rehearsed story; every fact handed over before the story is told helps weld it in place --- ask early.
  \item The cliff moment: the answerer has silently fixed the line they will not cross ('I can tell them this much and no more'); craft means making the step past the line feel survivable --- provocative but genuinely neutral questions aimed just past the cliff.
  \item Reserve rule: never spend known facts to confirm what you suspect; hold them and let the account hang itself.
  \item Chapter 14's warning: detection is not accusation --- acting on a read requires judgment about the relationship and the stakes (the CIA officer who heard a cluster from his own wife at a dinner table).
\end{distilled}
\begin{unique}
  \item The cliff-moment construct, the entrenchment-timing rule, and the reserve rule (hold known facts) are unique to B7 in this corpus.
\end{unique}
